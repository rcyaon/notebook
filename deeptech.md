# america still makes things

i think marinetti kind of nailed the spirit of american innovation way back in his *manifesto of futurism* in 1909. everyone at the time was freaked out by the machine age, convinced all this speed and technology was going to be the end of the world. marinetti just wasn't worried about it. "time and space died yesterday," he wrote, "we already live in the absolute, because we have created eternal, omnipresent speed." and that's kind of how america has always felt to me. it never really finds a reason to slow down. that same excitement about building fast is exactly what i see happening right now, and honestly nowhere more than in how we make things.

i recently went to yc sus, and between the software and deep tech talks, one thing kept echoing: software is cheaper than ever. in an age where agents can code, hardware engineers have found ways to make their tooling faster, and suddenly hardware processes are easier to use than they've ever been. and the place that shows up most is the factory floor. the stuff that used to make manufacturing slow and expensive is quietly getting easier.

this is the big one for me. america has found itself behind, sure, but the signs of potential are everywhere. fast iteration, manufacturing that molds itself to the companies it serves. it's becoming a new standard, even for young robotics companies still in their seed rounds, to in-house their pcb manufacturing and work in tight iteration cycles. it's something i hear echoed by basically every deep tech company worth hearing about. building the physical layer in-house, closer and faster, instead of shipping a design off and waiting weeks to find out if you were right.

there's also this clear sign of hardware engineers, electrical and mechanical both, engaging with their tooling more than ever. simulation is advancing so fast that tradeoffs aren't something you calculate and then wait weeks to see shipped back to you. they're something you can just check in the moment. as an electrical engineer, optimizing tradeoffs is the thing i chase most, and not every company has figured out how to do it well. so hearing about simulations where you can watch all these forces interplay directly, in real time, is honestly gorgeous. it collapses the distance between designing something and actually making it.

so that's basically it. everything exciting in deep tech right now, the robots, the agents, the fast iteration, eventually has to get built, and the part of that process that used to be the slowest is finally starting to move. for a while it felt like america had kind of forgotten how to make things, or handed that part off to someone else. but that's not really true anymore. america still makes things, and it's honestly a really good time to be one of the people making them.
