# america still makes things
on manufacturing: the slowest part of building is finally speeding up

*originally written on august 13, 2026*

i think marinetti kind of nailed the spirit of american innovation way back in his manifesto of futurism in 1909. everyone at the time was freaked out by the machine age, convinced all this speed and technology was going to be the end of the world. marinetti just wasn’t worried about it. “time and space died yesterday,” he wrote, “we already live in the absolute, because we have created eternal, omnipresent speed.” and that’s kind of how america has always felt to me. it never really finds a reason to slow down. that same excitement about building fast is exactly what i see happening right now, and honestly nowhere more than in how we make things.

the standard story goes the other way, of course. china makes things now. shenzhen turns a board around before an american team finishes its kickoff meeting, the supply chain lives there, and america is supposedly a decade behind and falling. i believed a soft version of that story for a while. i believe it less every month.

part of it is that the story itself is starting to crack when people actually go look. matt krisiloff wrote a great post[^1] after spending two weeks visiting startups in shanghai and shenzhen, and biotech is supposed to be the field where china’s lead is scariest. what he found was messier. costs for cutting-edge work were comparable to the us, the strongest american companies still looked stronger than the strongest chinese ones, and the advantages that are real came bundled with structural headwinds american startups just don’t have. his takeaway wasn’t that china is weak, but that the gap right now is nuance. and if that’s true in biotech, i think it’s even more true in hardware.

i recently went to yc sus, and between the software and deep tech talks, one thing kept echoing: software is cheaper than ever.[^2] in an age where agents can code, hardware engineers have found ways to make their tooling faster, and suddenly hardware processes are easier to use than they’ve ever been. and the place that shows up most is the factory floor. the stuff that used to make manufacturing slow and expensive is quietly getting easier.

this is the big one for me. the thing shenzhen actually had was never cheap labor, it was iteration speed. the loop between designing something and holding it. and american startups are rebuilding that loop domestically. it’s becoming a new standard, even for young robotics companies still in their seed rounds, to in-house their pcb manufacturing and work in tight iteration cycles. it’s something i hear echoed by basically every deep tech company worth hearing about. building the physical layer in-house, closer and faster, instead of shipping a design off and waiting weeks to find out if you were right.

and the part i find most underrated: nobody is doing it alone. one of the things krisiloff kept noticing in china was how open and collaborative the ecosystem felt, and the same texture is showing up in american deep tech. startups lend each other test setups, trade bringup war stories, publish their tooling, in-house a capability and then let the company down the street borrow it. open pdks in the semiconductor space mean a student team and a funded startup are literally iterating on the same process. it doesn’t feel like a hundred siloed companies each rediscovering manufacturing from scratch, but rather, one ecosystem compounding. and honestly, it’s also why the whole thing doesn’t have to read as a zero sum race in the first place. the ecosystems that collaborate, internally and across borders, are the ones that end up making the most things.

there’s also this clear sign of hardware engineers, electrical and mechanical both, engaging with their tooling more than ever. simulation is advancing so fast that tradeoffs aren’t something you calculate and then wait weeks to see shipped back to you. they’re something you can just check in the moment. as an electrical engineer, optimizing tradeoffs is the thing i chase most, and not every company has figured out how to do it well. so hearing about simulations where you can watch all these forces interplay directly, in real time, is honestly gorgeous. it collapses the distance between designing something and actually making it.

so that’s basically it. everything exciting in deep tech right now, the robots, the agents, the fast iteration, eventually has to get built, and the part of that process that used to be the slowest is finally starting to move. for a while it felt like america had kind of forgotten how to make things, or handed that part off to someone else. but that’s not really true anymore. america still makes things, and it’s honestly a really good time to be one of the people making them.
