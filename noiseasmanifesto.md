# noise as manifesto
on geese and the italian futurists

*originally posted on [substack](https://lenaphobia.substack.com), march 19, 2026*

i fell in love with geese a couple months ago. whether it’s cameron winter’s writing—laced with something that feels increasingly existential, especially in his song for war child, where references to kierkegaard start to surface—or their live performances, which never seem to land the same way twice instrumentally. 3d country had been sitting in my playlists for a while, but when *getting killed* finally came out, something clicked. hearing “taxes,” i felt genuinely understood for the first time in a long time.[^1]

that being said, i recently started exploring italian futurism after impulsively signing up for a one-credit class about it and it instantly captivated me. the aim to tear down institutions, for example, echoes in aaron swartz’s reimagining of the library as an open, public system—leaving no one behind by sharing knowledge with all. but there’s also something unsettling about how easily this rhetoric slides into repetition, how history seems ready to loop. that tension is probably something i’ll come back to. for now, it just sits as a kind of baseline skepticism.

what actually made me pause, though, was the artistic framework. the manifesto doesn’t just argue what art should be about, but how it should function, reimagining art as something active and participatory. a few ideas stuck with me:

• forcing active interpretation: the reader has to construct meaning instead of receiving it passively
• embracing chaos and fragmentation
• turning art into a manifesto for living

even if unintentionally, i see this reflected in geese.

winter has talked about wishing lyricism wasn’t so specific. in a way, that opens things up, creating space for interpretation. you can hear it in songs like “au pays du cocaine,” where earlier versions carried a more direct meaning than what was eventually released, and in “husbands,” which still feels unresolved. these songs resist closure and instead open a space for reflection, where meaning isn’t delivered neatly in a bow but constructed through listening. when asked about the meanings of their songs, geese never provides a definitive answer. this also feels like a kind of non-adherence to traditional songwriting, similar to how marinetti rejected syntax and coherence so audiences would have to construct meaning themselves.

this follows into the band’s relationship with chaos and fragmentation. futurism embraces disorder as a new kind of form, one that reflects the speed and over consumption of modern life. 3d country feels like an echo of this idea, an attempt to make something “noisy” but still narrative-driven, embracing chaos without fully collapsing into it. the tension between control and collapse is what gives the album its coherence.

there’s also winter’s style of singing, which adds spontaneous noises that feel reminiscent of marinetti’s poems,[^2] blurring the boundary between language and sound, communicating something that can’t be reduced to lyrics alone. moments like this, especially in “drinking age,” often feel more expressive than the words themselves, revealing something beyond what winter could articulate directly.

this tendency becomes even sharper in some of winter’s most discordant lyrics, particularly in “nina + field of cops.” in this song, the lyrics don’t just avoid clarity, but  actively resist cohesion. phrases feel stitched together without obvious connective logic, images appear abruptly and disappear just as quickly, and any sense of narrative continuity is constantly interrupted before it can stabilize. the result is disorienting, but not in a way that feels careless. instead, it creates a kind of productive confusion, where meaning is always just out of reach. talking about “nina,” especially, there’s a sense that the lyrics are circling something without ever directly naming it. lines feel emotionally charged but slippery, as if they’re operating more on association than description. rather than building toward a clear point, the song accumulates fragments, gestures toward meaning that never quite resolve.

this is where the connection to marinetti becomes harder to ignore. like marinetti’s experiments with *parole in libertà*, these songs strip away the usual structures that guide interpretation. what’s left is something closer to raw linguistic material, where words function as sounds and emotional signals as much as they do as carriers of meaning. the listener isn’t decoding a message so much as navigating a field of competing perspectives and images.

what makes this effective is that the songs never fully collapse into randomness. there’s still an underlying sense of intention, even if it’s obscured. that tension—between coherence and incoherence, meaning and its refusal—is what gives geese’s songs their weight. they don’t communicate in spite of their fragmentation, but through it.

[^1]: i love sisyphus55 and was so hyped to see [he made a video about geese](https://youtu.be/K0RZZzLu3K0?si=tj4i9iQP5Vwc27JB). he touched upon something i find so pertinent to the development of camus’ philosophy, which is the allegorical interpretation of guilt. in the bible, it’s described through taxes. like a kind of moral accounting where life becomes defined by an ongoing debt: to ultimately justify your existence in some way. but no matter how much you give or improve, it never quite feels like enough. there’s always something outstanding, some balance left unpaid. in that way, guilt isn’t just about specific actions. it becomes a constant condition, where you’re always owing more than you can ever fully repay.
[^2]: see *zang tumb tumb*.
